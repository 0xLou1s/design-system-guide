---
title: Measuring
description: Decide what to measure before you change anything.
---

Optimizing without measuring is guessing. You will spend an afternoon shaving
4kB off a stylesheet while a single unoptimized hero image costs 800kB, and the
page will feel exactly as slow as it did before.

Measure first, and measure the thing your users actually experience.

## Lab and field data

Two kinds of measurement, and they answer different questions.

**Lab data** comes from a synthetic test: one machine, one network profile, one
run. Lighthouse and WebPageTest produce this. It is reproducible, which makes it
good for catching regressions and for A/B-ing a change you just made.

**Field data** comes from real users on real devices — the Chrome User
Experience Report, or your own analytics. It is noisy and slow to update, but it
is the truth. Lab data says the page *can* be fast; field data says whether it
*is*.

Trust field data when the two disagree. A page that scores 98 in Lighthouse and
p75 of four seconds in the field is a slow page.

## Measure on a real device

The single most misleading thing you can do is test on the machine you develop
on. A recent laptop on office wifi is nothing like a three-year-old mid-range
Android phone on a congested mobile network, and the gap is widest exactly where
it hurts most — JavaScript parse and execute time.

Throttle deliberately:

- **CPU**: 4–6× slowdown in DevTools approximates a mid-range phone.
- **Network**: "Slow 4G" is a reasonable default. Test offline too if you have a
  service worker.
