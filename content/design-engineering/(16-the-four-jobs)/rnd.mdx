---

Building prototypes of new things — visualising and testing products that do not exist yet.

These roles are rarer and found mostly at larger companies with the budget to explore.

---

## What the work is

```text
prototypes of unreleased products
proving out an interaction nobody has built
exploring a new input modality or platform
testing ideas with real users, early
internal tools for other researchers
```

The output is usually **knowledge**, not shipped software. You build to find out whether something works, then you report what you learned and the prototype is discarded.

---

## What it demands

Baytaş, from ten years in R&D: *"It's the Wild West — you have to draw quickly."*

**Speed over polish.** Chapter 11's disposable prototyping, as the whole job rather than a technique.

**Ambiguity tolerance.** Often there is no spec, no designer, and no certainty the thing should exist. You are finding out.

**Breadth.** New platforms and unfamiliar APIs constantly. Depth in one framework matters less than picking things up fast.

**Communication.** A prototype that proves something and convinces nobody has failed. Much of the value is in how you present what you found.

---

## The honest warning

Worth repeating plainly: **R&D is the first thing cut when companies face distress.**

It is expensive, its returns are uncertain and slow, and it is easy to defer. When budgets tighten, exploratory teams go before product teams.

Two implications:

```text
keep shipping skills sharp    you may need to move to product
prefer companies where R&D    is close to the product roadmap
```

R&D that feeds a real roadmap survives downturns better than R&D that reports to a vision.

---

## Who it suits

If you like starting things more than finishing them, are comfortable when the goal is unclear, and enjoy learning a new API every month — this is the most interesting of the four.

If you want to see your work in users' hands, it will frustrate you. Most of what you build will never ship, by design.
