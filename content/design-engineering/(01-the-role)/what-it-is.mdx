The definitions worth knowing, because each one names a different part of the job.

---

## The sharp one

Baytaş, writing in Design Discipline:

> A design engineer is an engineer with design skills.

What makes it useful is what he attaches to it. A design engineer:

- does not need designers designing what to build — they can take the inputs designers take and deliver the outputs engineers deliver
- communicates efficiently with designers and **contributes to** design work
- builds with production-grade materials for decisions that cannot be experienced in any other material
- builds tools that make designing easier, better, faster

The third point is the one engineers underrate. Some design decisions genuinely cannot be made in Figma — an animation curve, a drag gesture, how a layout behaves at every width between breakpoints. They can only be judged in the real material. That makes them yours.

---

## The workflow one

Vercel describes what changes operationally:

> Design engineers skip the traditional handoff process.

A designer sketches the start and iterates with a design engineer in Figma or code to produce the final thing. Idea to production, no wall to throw work over.

This is worth sitting with. The handoff is not improved, it is **removed** — because the person who would have received it is in the room while the decision is being made.

---

## The organisational one

Google, who have run a UX Engineering career track for years, frame it as:

> The glue between design and engineering.

One of their UX Engineers describes the day-to-day: "You'll never have the same day. One day you might design an animation for an app, and the next day, you might write a machine learning algorithm."

---

## What it is not

**Not a frontend developer who uses Figma.** Opening design files is not the skill. Making the decisions inside them is.

**Not a designer who learned React.** That is the other entry path — same destination, opposite starting point.

**Not a unicorn or a generalist.** Anna Lefour's framing is right: it lives at the intersection of visual design and front-end development, **not as a generalist compromise, but as its own thing**.

The distinction matters for how you learn. A generalist spreads thin across both fields. A design engineer goes deep on a specific overlap: the front of the front-end — HTML, CSS, component states, accessibility, motion — plus the judgement to know what is right.
