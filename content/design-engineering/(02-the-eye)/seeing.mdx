Before the principles: how to look. These techniques work by removing information, which is what lets you see structure instead of detail.

---

## Squint

Blur your eyes until the detail disappears and only shapes and values remain.

What survives the blur is your actual hierarchy. Detail vanishes; weight, grouping and structure do not. If everything reads as one grey mass, you have no hierarchy — whatever you intended.

This takes two seconds and catches more than any checklist. It is the single most useful habit in this chapter.

---

## The five-second test

Show someone the screen for five seconds, take it away, ask what it was for and what they would do next.

If they cannot answer, the entry point is not working. This mirrors how people actually arrive at a screen: fast, distracted, not reading.

---

## Flip it

Mirror the layout horizontally, or turn it upside down. Both break your familiarity with the content, so you see composition rather than meaning.

Balance problems become obvious when you can no longer read the words.

---

## Come back tomorrow

The cheapest technique and the one people skip. Leave it a day and look again.

Everything you rushed will be visible, and you will not be able to unsee it. You are not a different person the next day — you have just lost the context that was papering over the gaps.

---

## Compare directly

Put your version next to the one you admire, at the same size, on the same screen.

Differences that are invisible in isolation are obvious side by side. This is why the copy-work in chapter 17 is effective: it forces the comparison, and the comparison is where the learning happens.

---

## Why these work

All five do the same thing — they remove your ability to compensate.

When you look at your own work normally, you see what you meant. You know the heading is the heading, so you perceive it as prominent whether or not it is. Squinting, flipping, and waiting all strip that knowledge away and leave you with what is actually on screen.

Getting in the habit of not trusting your first look is most of what "having an eye" consists of.
