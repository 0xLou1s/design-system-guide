Balance is how visual weight is distributed. When it is wrong, a layout feels like it is tipping — and people notice without knowing why.

---

## Visual weight

Weight is not size. An element is heavy in proportion to how much attention it pulls:

```text
heavy    large, dark, saturated, dense, high contrast, images
light    small, pale, desaturated, sparse, low contrast, whitespace
```

A small dark button can outweigh a large pale card. A photograph outweighs almost anything.

---

## Symmetry is not the goal

Symmetrical layouts are balanced by default, and often dull. Most good interfaces are **asymmetrically** balanced: a heavy element on one side offset by several light ones on the other.

Think of a seesaw. One large weight near the centre balances several small weights further out.

The practical version in interfaces: a bold heading on the left balanced by a dense block of metadata on the right, or an image balanced by a column of text.

---

## Where it goes wrong

**Everything crowds one side.** Usually a sidebar carrying all the visual weight while the main content is pale, so the page feels like it is leaning.

**Top-heavy pages.** A huge hero followed by thin content. Common in marketing pages built section by section without looking at the whole.

**Orphaned elements.** A single button floating in space with nothing to anchor it.

**Uneven density.** One region tightly packed, another sparse, with no reason for the difference.

---

## Checking it

Flip the layout horizontally, or squint at it. Both remove your ability to read, which is what lets you see weight.

The question is simply: does one region feel heavier than the other, and is that intentional?

Deliberate imbalance is a legitimate tool — it creates tension and directs attention. The problem is only ever *accidental* imbalance.

---

## In practice

Balance is the principle you will apply least consciously, because it is mostly a consequence of the others being right.

If hierarchy is clear, grouping is correct, and alignment holds, balance usually follows. When it does not, the fix is normally redistributing space rather than moving elements — which puts you in chapter 5.
