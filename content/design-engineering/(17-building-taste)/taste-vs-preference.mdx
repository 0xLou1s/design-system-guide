---
title: "17.3 Taste vs preference"
description: "Building Taste · Section 17.3"
---

Taste curdles into preference when it stops being about the user. This is the distinction that keeps a developing eye useful instead of merely opinionated.

---

## The difference

```text
preference    wanting an interface to be minimal
taste         knowing a dense table serves someone scanning
              200 rows, and building that instead of the
              thing that would photograph well
```

The question is never "do I like it." It is "does this help the person using it."

Interfaces that win design awards and frustrate daily users come from getting this backwards — optimising for how the work looks to other designers rather than how it works for the person in it.

---

## Where it shows up

**Density.** Covered in 5.4, and the clearest case. Generous whitespace is pleasant to look at and actively worse for someone comparing forty rows.

**Animation.** Motion that delights on first view is friction on the five-hundredth (7.1). Your experience of it is not the user's.

**Novelty.** A creative navigation pattern is more interesting to build than a standard one, and worse for everyone who already knows how the standard one works.

**Minimalism.** Removing labels and relying on icons looks cleaner and measurably reduces comprehension.

In every case the thing that feels like better design to the person making it is worse for the person using it.

---

## How to tell which you are doing

Ask whether you can state the user-facing reason:

```text
"the spacing is too tight"                 preference
"the group boundaries are not readable
 because inner and outer gaps match"       taste

"this animation should be slower"          preference
"the exit is faster than the entrance,
 which makes dismissal feel unfinished"    taste
```

If you cannot name the effect on the person using it, you are expressing a preference. That is allowed — just do not spend credibility on it (12.2).

---

## The other failure

Over-correcting is also possible: refusing to have aesthetic judgement because it feels unjustifiable.

Aesthetics are real. An interface that feels considered earns trust, and trust changes behaviour. "It looks more professional" is a legitimate argument when the product's credibility matters.

The discipline is not eliminating aesthetic judgement — it is knowing when you are exercising it, and being honest about that rather than dressing it up as usability.

---

## Where this leaves you

As your eye develops you will have more opinions and be right more often. Both of those are true at once, and the second does not follow automatically from the first.

Keep asking who it is for. That question is the difference between taste that makes you valuable and taste that makes you difficult.
