Easing carries more of the feel than duration, and receives far less attention.

The same 200ms transition can feel mechanical, natural or bouncy depending entirely on the curve.

---

## The three you need

```css
--ease-out:   cubic-bezier(0, 0, 0.2, 1);    /* entering */
--ease-in:    cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 1, 1);    /* exiting */
--ease-inout: cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 0.2, 1);  /* moving between positions */
```

**Entering eases out.** Fast at the start, settling at the end. The element arrives quickly and comes to rest — which reads as responsive.

**Exiting eases in.** Slow at the start, accelerating away. It leaves decisively.

**Moving eases in-out.** Something travelling from one position to another accelerates and decelerates, as physical objects do.

---

## Never use linear for UI

`transition: 200ms linear` reads as mechanical because nothing in the physical world moves at a constant velocity with instant start and stop.

Linear is correct for exactly two things: continuous rotation (spinners) and progress bars. Everything with a beginning and an end needs a curve.

Worth knowing: CSS `ease` (the default) is `cubic-bezier(0.25, 0.1, 0.25, 1)` — slightly eased both ends. It is acceptable and slightly generic. `ease-out` is usually a better default for interface work.

---

## Why ease-out feels responsive

The perceptual reason is worth internalising: most of the visible movement happens in the first few frames.

The element is *almost* where it is going within the first 30% of the duration, then settles. The user's eye registers arrival early, so the interaction feels immediate even though the animation is still finishing.

`ease-in` does the opposite — nothing appears to happen for the first third — which is why it feels laggy on entrances and correct on exits.

---

## Springs

Springs are described by physics (stiffness, damping, mass) rather than duration and curve.

Their real advantage is **interruption**. A spring that is mid-animation when the user acts again continues naturally from its current velocity. A CSS transition restarts, which produces a visible stutter.

Use springs for anything draggable, rapidly toggleable, or gesture-driven — sheets, drag-and-drop, pull-to-refresh. For a dropdown opening, a spring is more machinery than the problem needs.

```ts
// Framer Motion
transition={{ type: "spring", stiffness: 400, damping: 30 }}
```

Higher stiffness is faster; higher damping means less bounce. Overshoot is appropriate for playful, physical interactions and wrong for most product UI — a settings panel that bounces reads as unserious.
